# Q1 2025 pricing – summary and recommendation

## Recommendation

I recommend raising prices in all six regions for Q1 2025, by between 2% and 10%. Based on the demand forecast, this adds about **$10.7M in margin** for the quarter (from $107M to $118M), while revenue stays roughly the same.

| Region | Current price | Q1 price | Change | Extra margin (Q1) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Midwest | $1,003 | $1,103 | +10% | $3.4M |
| Northeast | $927 | $1,019 | +10% | $3.0M |
| Gulf Coast | $957 | $1,052 | +10% | $2.0M |
| Great Lakes | $1,102 | $1,165 | +6% | $1.2M |
| Southeast | $968 | $1,058 | +9% | $1.0M |
| West | $1,091 | $1,117 | +2% | $0.1M |
| Total | | | | $10.7M |

Prices are $ per ton. Current price = Q4 2024 average.

## Why

At current prices we can't supply all the demand. For Q1, the forecast shows about 19,800 tons more demand than the plants can produce, mostly in Midwest and Northeast. Raising prices there costs us very little volume that we could actually deliver.

![Q1 demand vs capacity, and extra margin by region](SUMMARY_chart.png)

Looking at four years of data, a 1% price increase reduces volume by about 2%. With a variable cost of $600/ton, margin is highest at around $1,165/ton. Four of the six regions are currently between $930 and $1,000.

We would sell around 9% fewer tons, but each ton earns more, so total margin goes up.

I limited the increases to 10% this quarter and kept each region within prices it has charged before. We don't have data on how customers react above those levels.

## How reliable is this

- When tested on 2023–24, the demand forecast was off by about 5% on average. Simply repeating last month's number was off by about 11%.
- Even if demand turns out weaker, or customers are more price-sensitive than estimated, the increase still adds between $6M and $17M in Q1.
- A few data issues were fixed before the analysis: mislabelled regions, duplicate rows, two values with a misplaced decimal point, and some missing entries.

## Risks

- Competitor reaction isn't modelled. The numbers assume they keep their prices.
- Increases need to be communicated well to protect customer relationships.
- Price sensitivity is based on historical data. A trial in one or two regions would confirm it.

## Next steps

1. Review the proposed prices with the regional sales leads.
2. Start with Midwest and Northeast.
3. Track actual sales against the forecast each month, and decide Q2 prices in March.
